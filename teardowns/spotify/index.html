Spotify: personalisation as a product, not a feature

A teardown of how listening data becomes Discover Weekly, Wrapped and the feeling that the app knows you, and where the same loop can quietly narrow taste.

PersonalisationEngagement loopsTrust and controlExperiment design

How to read this. Independent analysis from public sources: company results, Spotify's own newsroom and published commentary. Numbers are from Q3 2026 unless dated otherwise.

1 · The product in numbers

A huge free base, a large paying one

Monthly active users788Mup 11% year on year
Premium subscribers306Mup 9% year on year
Ad-supported users499Mmonthly active
Revenue€4.97Bup 16% year on year

Source: Spotify Q3 2026 results as reported by Hypebot and Music Business Worldwide.

By my arithmetic, about 39% of monthly users pay (306M divided by 788M). The rest is both Spotify's advertising audience and its pool of future subscribers. Personalisation is what links the two: the better the app knows someone, the more reasons they have to stay, and eventually to pay.

2 · The core loop

Listening teaches the app, and the app teaches listening

1ListenPlays, skips, saves and playlist adds all count as signals about taste.
2ModelSpotify describes a Taste Profile built from what you play and how you play it.
3ServeThe profile shapes Home, Daily Mix and the Monday Discover Weekly playlist.
4ReflectWrapped turns a year of listening into a story about you.
5SharePeople post their Wrapped, which markets the product to friends.

Personalisation is the product, and sharing is the growth channel. Both run on the same data.

3 · What works, what strains

The ritual is strong. The control is uneven.

What it does well

  • A weekly ritual. Discover Weekly has landed every Monday for more than a decade, which gives people a reason to open the app on a schedule.
  • Visible engagement lift. In 2019 data, Spotify said Discover Weekly users streamed more than twice as long as non-users. That is correlation, since people who like discovery choose it, but it shows the feature is central.
  • Personalisation as marketing. Wrapped had 245M users engage with it in 2024 and doubles as a year-end ad people post for free.
  • Making the model visible. A Taste Profile beta shows Premium users in the US how Spotify reads their taste, and people can already exclude a track from it.

Where it strains

  • Taste can narrow. Critics argue that recommendation loops reinforce what people already like, so preferences harden into identity.
  • Control is uneven. Genre controls on Discover Weekly and prompt-based playlists are Premium or beta features, so free users shape less.
  • Reflection is annual. Wrapped is a great mirror, but it arrives once a year. In the other eleven months people get no view of how their taste is changing.
4 · What I would test

Two changes, each with a way to be wrong

DiscoveryRetention

Taste drift: a mid-year mirror

Wrapped is loved because it makes taste visible. I would bring a small version of that moment into the year, with a reason to explore.

Idea
Once a quarter, show a short in-app card: how your listening moved, three artists new to you, and one tap to get more or less like them.
Hypothesis
A recurring reflection moment increases exploration and gives long-time repeat listeners a reason to try new music, which supports longer-term retention.
Primary metric
Share of listening time on artists first streamed in the last 90 days.
Secondary
Saves and playlist adds from the card, 90-day retention of heavy repeat listeners, shares of the card.
Guardrails
Skip rate, session length, and a short satisfaction survey. If the card feels like an interruption, it should lose.
Test design
A/B test with a holdout among users with at least a year of history. Read exploration at four weeks and retention at 12.
Risk
Reflection can feel like surveillance. The card should show only what the person already did and let them dismiss it permanently.
ControlTrust

A session-only "not in this mood" signal

Spotify already lets people exclude a track from their Taste Profile. That is a permanent edit. Much of the time, the reason for skipping is a mood, not a verdict on taste.

Idea
On any recommended item, offer a one-tap "not right now". It steers the rest of the session and leaves the long-term profile alone.
Hypothesis
If people worry that a skip will pollute their profile, a safe temporary signal increases honest feedback and improves the next few recommendations.
Primary metric
Share of recommended tracks played to completion within a session after the signal is used.
Secondary
Signal usage rate, saves per session, edits made to the Taste Profile.
Guardrails
Seven-day return rate and long-term recommendation quality. Session steering must not leak into the profile.
Test design
A/B test on one Home shelf first. Before building, I would run interviews to check whether the fear of polluting the profile is real, since that belief is my assumption.
Risk
Another control adds clutter. It only ships if usage is high enough to justify the space it takes.
5 · How I would measure it

Metrics to watch, and how each can mislead

MetricWhy it mattersWatch out for
Discovery rate (listening on newly found artists)Shows whether personalisation widens taste or narrows it.Novelty for its own sake can lower satisfaction.
Skip rate and completion rate by surfaceThe fastest signal of whether recommendations fit.Skips can mean mood, not dislike.
Weekly return to Discover WeeklyMeasures the ritual directly.Self-selection: fans of discovery return more by nature.
Cohort retention (D30, D90)Shows if personalisation holds people over time.Needs cohorts by tenure, not blended averages.
Free to Premium conversionThe business outcome of engagement.Upsell prompts can damage the habit that drives conversion.
The takeaway for a PM

Spotify's moat is not the catalogue, which rivals can match. It is the sense of being understood. That makes trust the thing to protect: the more the product learns, the more control people need to feel they can steer it.
