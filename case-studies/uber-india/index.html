Uber Auto in India: fewer cancellations without breaking driver economics

When a ride is accepted and then cancelled, the rider loses trust in the whole app. This case study asks how to cut those cancellations in a zero-commission market where the usual levers are gone.

MarketplaceTrust and safetyTrade-offsExperiment design

How to read this. An independent case study using public reporting only. I have no access to Uber's data, so the numbers I propose are assumptions, labelled as such.

1 · Context

The business model changed under the product

In 2025 Uber moved its auto-rickshaw service in India from a per-trip commission to a subscription: drivers pay a flat fee, and Uber takes no cut of the fare. Rapido and Namma Yatri had already done the same. Uber also said it suggests a fare, but the final fare is agreed between driver and rider, and that it does not control how the ride is carried out.

The shift matters for product design. Under commission, Uber earned when a ride finished, so completion was its own incentive. Under a flat fee, a cancelled ride costs Uber no revenue directly. The pressure to fix it now comes from rider trust, competition and regulation.

2 · The problem

A cancelled ride is a broken promise

A rider sees "driver found" and stops looking at other options. If the driver then cancels, asks for extra money or does not turn up, the rider has lost time and the platform has lost trust. Drivers have reasons too: an unattractive destination, a fare that does not fit the distance, or a pickup that is hard to reach.

Problem statement

How might we raise the share of accepted rides that actually complete, while keeping driver earnings per hour intact? The goal is to align both sides before the ride is accepted, not to punish after it is cancelled.

Two users, two views

Rider (illustrative): a commuter in a hurry who has turned down other apps because Uber found a driver. Driver (illustrative): an auto driver who pays a daily or weekly fee and needs rides that make the fee worthwhile. Uber has already moved to show drivers the destination before they accept, which tells us mismatch is a known cause.

4 · Recommendation

Align before the ride, reward reliability after it

v1 scope

Reliable ride

What ships
  • Before a driver is locked in, both sides see the suggested fare and the destination. Fare changes after that need the rider's approval in the app, not a conversation at the kerb.
  • A pickup confirmation step: the rider checks the pin and the driver sees clear instructions, so a hard-to-find pickup does not end in a cancel.
  • A driver reliability record that counts only cancellations the driver caused. Riders who did not show up, or wrong pins, are excluded.
  • Reliable drivers get better dispatch priority, shown as a benefit, never as a penalty.
Non-goals
No fees on drivers, no change to the subscription price, and no change to how riders are charged.
Key assumption
Most post-acceptance cancellations come from mismatched expectations on fare, destination or pickup, which a better flow can fix. If most are driver opportunism, matching and incentives matter more.

6 · How I would test it

Test by zone and time, not by user

A marketplace has a trap: riders and drivers share supply, so a rider-level A/B test spills over, and the control group benefits from the treatment. I would use a switchback test, where a zone alternates between the new flow and the old one by time block, or compare matched cities.

  • Start: one or two zones in one city for about four weeks, comparing like for like on time of day and day of week.
  • Read: completion rate first, then driver earnings per hour. Both must hold.
  • Stop rule: if driver supply or earnings per hour fall beyond a set threshold, pause and review before rollout.
7 · Risks and open questions

What could break this

Risks

  • Drivers may read the reliability record as surveillance. It needs clear rules and a way to dispute.
  • Locking the fare early may reduce flexibility that both sides value in a negotiated model.
  • Local rules on cancellations differ by state, so one design may not fit every city.

First questions for internal data

  • What share of cancellations come after acceptance, and for which stated reasons?
  • Do cancels cluster by destination, time of day or fare gap?
  • How does the subscription tier relate to a driver's willingness to accept?
